2046

It's 2046. A bakery gets an order for 5,000 pastry boxes. By tomorrow.

The owner photographs the counter, enters the box dimensions, and orders a robot.

By morning, a compact machine is folding boxes and packing pastries. Its arms fit beneath the shelves; its gripper handles the paper without crushing the food.

Next week, those same components will become a different machine, doing a different job.

What if ordering a robot started with the task, and the body came afterward?

The world · 2046

Twenty years after robotics decided to copy the human body, it still hasn't questioned that choice.

The field settled on the humanoid early, because a world built for people seemed to call for robots shaped like people. Every step since has deepened that commitment: the training data comes from human demonstrations and video of people at work, and investment and benchmarks have followed the same shape.

By 2046 the robot brain is effectively solved, but the body has barely been questioned, and it has become the main thing holding robotics back.

Simulation finally matches reality.

World models can build a detailed environment from a scan or a short description, and physics engines are now accurate enough that what a robot learns in simulation carries over when it is deployed.

Yet the field kept training the same body.

Tools that could have explored any shape were spent perfecting one, because the data, the supply chain and everyone's expectations were already built around the humanoid.

A copied body inherits human limits.

A humanoid designed to work in our spaces also takes on our reach, grip, senses and reflexes, so it can match a person but was never meant to surpass one.

The most valuable work lies beyond those limits.

Repairing the inside of a fusion reactor, assembling structures in orbit, operating at scales finer than a surgeon's hand, and harvesting a field around the clock are all jobs where a human-shaped body gets in the way.

The body is still chosen before training starts.

Engineers pick the motors, joints and sensors by hand, often borrowing from our own anatomy, and RL then learns to make the best of whatever it was given.

What if the body were trained along with the brain?

Thesis

The human body is a remarkable generalist, which is exactly why it makes a poor template for specialized work.

Evolution shaped our bodies to survive in many environments, not to excel at any single task. Every part of that design is a compromise between speed and strength, precision and reach, and senses tuned to what our ancestors needed to notice. When robots copy that design, they copy the compromises along with it.

Overview

We propose a generalized process for creating task-specific robots. Given details on a specific task, we iterate over various modular combinations in order to determine which robot design performs the best. Then, we use reinforcement learning in order to create a specialized model for the task. A hybrid pipeline of mass production and skilled human assembly then creates the final robot. Despite limitations of task-specific robots when compared to generalized designs, we achieve positive unit economics as well as sustainability through a contract model and reusable components. Ultimately, we hope to lessen the barrier to entry for manufacturing, reinvigorating domestic manufacturing and innovation.

Our premise

Sim-to-real will be solved in the future.

  1. World models are able to generate 3D worlds from prompts (Atlas, Marble from World Labs, etc.).
  2. RL solves the gap, meaning that physics engines like MuJoCo plus AGI with near-infinite capabilities are able to simulate tons of data.
  3. Smarter models mean less data is required: AGI generates smarter robotics frameworks with more efficient actuation, policies, etc. for fewer actuators and degrees of freedom.
  4. Compute power and vertical scale are only increasing, which allows for custom world or environment generation and physics engine accuracy.

Simulation

In our simulation, we begin by evaluating a "parent configuration," which is a well-understood robot design that best matches the requisite task. From there, we evaluate hundreds of modularized variants (candidate configurations) from our parts library that can interact with the customer's task. Before we run any specific simulations, we utilize a physical constraint model to reject designs that exceed torque, payload, connector load limits, etc.

Training and ranking

For each finalist, a model writes a reward function suitable for that variant's structure. The control policies of the finalists are refined through thousands of randomized simulations involving the requisite task. Finalists are ranked on specific criteria chosen by the customer (e.g. task success, cycle time, dollar cost) to find the winner. Real-world results from each deployment are fed back into our database and module calibrations, so we can make better future recommendations with our model.

RL environment

The agent learns the robot's body.

State
The current robot configuration.
Action
Adding a sensor or actuator.
Reward
The robot's performance on the task in simulation.

Each step, the agent changes the robot, the new design is tested in simulation, and the reward decides which changes are kept.

Robot framework

Our design is highly modular, so once our RL framework settles on the best arrangement of components, we simply put the pieces together. Any of our designs can be made from combinations of core components, joints, structural links, locomotive devices, end effectors, and sensors, all of which can be linked together with a universal connector. This enables a broad range of capabilities, both from the same manufacturing process and from the same instantiated robot.

Manufacturing

We mass-produce components for our project, and we would aim to own the entire vertical stack when it comes to these mechanical components. Our core techniques are CNC machining, injection molding, and PCB assembly. Parts of this process are highly automatable; in other parts, though, it truly is more efficient to hire skilled human labor.

To assemble our robots, we simply join these components with universal connectors. This is highly automatable.

How it works

design→train→transfer→deploy→reuse

How can we predict the behavior of a newly assembled robot?

Known components make the modeling problem smaller. We reuse their physical descriptions, then calibrate the interactions introduced by the new assembly.

Technical details

The assembly graph determines geometry, mass distribution, and joint connections. Component models supply actuator behavior; short calibration routines estimate remaining errors from friction, connector flexibility, and tolerances. Stronger AI could automate experiment selection and model correction.

Research: Agile legged locomotion in reconfigurable modular robots, PNAS, 2026, demonstrates physical modular assemblies with learned control.

Does every robot body in a new environment need to learn from scratch?

Changing the body does not erase all useful knowledge. A controller can reuse skills while adapting to the new arrangement.

Technical details

Condition the policy on an assembly graph containing module properties and connectivity. Share parameters across compatible modules, pretrain across many simulated configurations, and fine-tune unfamiliar assemblies. Initially restrict transfer claims to a defined component and task family.

Research: Learning Modular Robot Control Policies, IEEE Transactions on Robotics, 2023, demonstrates shared policies and generalization to unseen modular designs.

How can we find good designs of new robots at a low cost?

The system learns which designs deserve expensive testing. Experience from earlier projects narrows the search for the next one.

Technical details

Reject physically infeasible assemblies first, screen candidates with inexpensive evaluations, then allocate longer training to promising or uncertain designs. Evaluate learning curves so a potentially superior body is not rejected merely because it learns slowly. RL trains behavior; design search can use RL, Bayesian optimization, or evolutionary methods.

Research: Transform2Act, ICLR, 2022, demonstrates experience sharing across design and control. This is a conference paper.

Why should a robot optimized in simulation work in reality?

Simulation needs to capture the variations that affect successful control. Robust policies can tolerate some modeling error, with physical testing identifying consequential mismatches.

Technical details

Vary friction, contact, actuator response, sensor noise, and delay during training. Give the deployed policy only measurements its actual sensors provide. Test shortlisted designs under held-out conditions and physically validate them; use calibration or targeted post-training when transfer falls short.

Research: IndustReal, RSS, 2023, demonstrates bounded transfer for contact-rich assembly. It supports this mechanism, not universal sim-to-real transfer.

Economics

Despite the typical disadvantages of task-specific hardware when compared to generalized approaches, we achieve positive unit economics through our business model. Our biggest markets would be specialized tasks in manufacturing, agriculture, and warehouse automation, but we also see significant use cases for small businesses. We are contracted with companies, charge a fee when implementing a new robot design, and implement a subscription model to charge for new instances and to lease the models' components. We achieve economies of scale by reusing components for new applications. In doing so, we also maintain sustainability and circumvent the scarcity of the requisite rare earth magnets.

Economic outlook

Stronger AI and cheaper robotic adaptation could expand automation beyond large, standardized production lines. Smaller businesses and shorter production runs could become economical to automate, increasing productive capacity and potentially lowering prices. Benefits would be uneven: routine manual workers could face displacement even as production grows and new technical roles emerge.

Source: World Bank

Factories and warehouses

Specialized machines should remain strongest where tasks are repetitive and throughput matters most. Humanoids may be competitive where frequent task-switching and compatibility with human-designed workstations outweigh their mechanical complexity. Modular robots occupy the middle: specialized performance with lower costs of adapting to changing products, layouts, and payloads.

Agriculture and small businesses

Task-specific machines are attractive when their bodies and tools can match particular crops or workflows. However, seasonality, environmental variation, and limited operating hours can weaken the economics. Leasing and reusable equipment could make automation accessible without requiring each business to purchase a permanent custom system.

Homes

There is unlikely to be one winning form. Dedicated appliances remain attractive for frequent, narrow tasks, while humanoids or wheeled manipulators could handle broader assistance. Low utilization and highly variable environments make general household robotics a harder economic problem than continuous industrial work.

Adoption sequence

Further automation will likely begin with bounded tasks such as material handling, machine tending, palletizing, and standardized packing. Variable assembly, delicate harvesting, and broader household work become more attractive as perception, manipulation, and recovery improve. These transitions will happen task by task; there is no universal date when specialized robots make humanoids uneconomical.

Unit economics

The relevant comparison is cost per successful unit of work, including equipment, installation, training, financing, maintenance, downtime, and human intervention. Illustratively, a system costing $30,000 annually and producing 5,000 human-equivalent productive hours costs $6 per equivalent hour; at 1,000 hours, it costs $30. Adoption in lower-wage markets therefore requires greater utilization, lower costs, or additional quality and productivity advantages.

Our place in this market

The proposed startup targets applications where conventional customization is expensive but a general-purpose robot provides unnecessary capabilities. Standardized components and reusable RL policies reduce the cost of configuring specialized machines. The framework can extend across industries through different assemblies, although each application still requires suitable hardware and validation.

Business model

Customers pay an implementation fee for configuration, training, installation, and validation, followed by a recurring fee for deployed robots, component leasing, software, and service. This lowers customer upfront investment. Positive unit economics depend on those payments covering equipment financing, adaptation, maintenance, and support, not simply on using a subscription model.

Scale and resource efficiency

Common modules create manufacturing economies of scale, while shared learning reduces repeated development work. Returned components can be refurbished and redeployed when doing so costs less than replacement. This increases hardware utilization and reduces demand for new motors and magnets, easing (but not eliminating) exposure to critical-material constraints.

Source: IEA

Impact

Our model will dramatically boost manufacturing capability, especially domestically. This enables the country, for the first time regarding matters that are not related to national security, to circumvent the recent crisis regarding cost of labor in the US. Our priorities include replacing or assisting with dangerous manufacturing and first-response jobs.

Ultimately, we also believe that we will enable new innovations. Since humanoid robots simply match human capabilities, while our robots exceed human capabilities, we aim to push the frontier of what is possible through engineering.

In terms of scale, we also foresee a recursive element. Implementing our own robots in order to optimize our manufacturing process will improve our efficiency.

Our vision

We believe in a world where we've created a pipeline to generalize the creation of task-specific robots, for any task. Task-specific robots are positioned to solve any automatable challenge; world models and compute-enhanced physics engines solve our data gap, and we use RL and AGI pipelines to create optimized robot designs that can do any task.

These robots are orderable for manufacturing, small businesses, daily life, defense, and more. At scale, task-specific robots automate tasks faster and cheaper than human labor, pushing the United States above China in the race for domestic manufacturing.
